When is
wealth liquid?Gold and Household Financial Constraints in India

A family can own a home, land or jewellery and still struggle to pay its bills. We study which wealth households can use—and how their spending responds when income changes.

Vivek Gupta · Fiorella Pizzolon · Aditi Singh

Read latest paper ↗
Wealth is not cash in hand

Owning wealth does not make it ready to spend. A home provides shelter and gold stores value. Paying today’s bills requires cash, or a way to turn an asset into cash.

Why focus on gold rather than land/house?

Gold can provide cash more quickly and with less paperwork than a home. Selling or borrowing against housing involves finding a buyer or lender, checking ownership documents and valuing the property. Both assets can be collateral, but they differ in how readily households can use them.

India has an established market for loans against gold. The World Gold Council describes this as a route to short-term finance. The Household Finance Committee documents gold’s broad acceptance as collateral and barriers to borrowing against real estate.

Predictors and model tuning

Current prediction specifications describe age groups rather than the household head: counts below 18, at 18–24, at 25–64 and at 65+, men and median age among 25–64-year-olds, education, religion, caste, employment type, state and district. Financial predictors include consumption, livestock, tractors and cars, bank accounts, saving in shares and mutual funds, borrowing sources and purposes, and vehicle counts. Wealth models additionally include debt service. The headline excludes indicators identified as non-comparable across surveys, including house ownership, two-wheeler ownership, fixed deposits and gold saving. The older AIDIS characterization retains head-based demographics; a complete current predictor-by-HtM summary has not yet been exported.

The clickable method descriptions report the training and tuning choices. Figures show aggregate distributions; the website contains no household predictions.

03The classification

Low liquidity has more than one face.

Hand-to-mouth households have little liquid wealth relative to income. Some also have little net illiquid wealth; others own assets that do not readily pay for current spending. The distinction follows Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (2014).

From the idea to the measurement

Why half a month’s income?

A household living from paycheck to paycheck can still have cash on the day it is interviewed. If income arrives at the start of the month and is spent steadily, cash falls from the full paycheck to zero before the next payment. Its average balance over the month is half the paycheck.

That average gives us a benchmark for identifying limited liquidity. We compare survey balances with half of monthly income, following Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (2014, Section II). The survey balance is a snapshot within the payment cycle, so the rule approximates living hand to mouth; it does not observe each household’s spending path.

How the rule treats savings and debt

The implemented rule uses net liquid wealth: liquid assets minus liquid debts. For nonnegative balances, a household is hand-to-mouth when this is no more than half its monthly income. A larger balance places it in the non-hand-to-mouth group under this benchmark.

For borrowers, the rule measures remaining borrowing room. It assumes an unsecured credit limit equal to one month’s income and counts a borrower as hand-to-mouth when net liquid wealth plus that limit is no more than half monthly income. Negative net liquid wealth alone is not enough.

Illiquid wealth then separates poor from wealthy hand-to-mouth households. The gold options below change which resources enter each part of the balance sheet.

Gold as collateral in IndiaAccess, speed and amount

Gold offers an established route from household wealth to short-term cash. The Household Finance Committee (2017, Sections 1.4 and 4.1) reports limited mortgage participation despite substantial housing wealth, alongside broad acceptance of gold as collateral.

Speed matters when an expense cannot wait. For example, State Bank of India’s gold-loan information says applications are normally sanctioned on the same day. This illustrates the accessibility of gold lending; a home’s reported market value does not by itself establish access to an equally quick loan.

The capacity comparison asks whether the amount is sufficient. Gold stays in illiquid wealth, while a specified fraction of its value is compared with the liquid shortfall. This measures potential borrowing capacity, rather than a household’s observed decision to borrow.

The preferred combined definitionHousing quality + gold capacity

The preferred definition treats gold as illiquid and combines the ₹100,000 real-estate screen, at least two of three low-infrastructure markers, and a 75% pledgeable gold fraction with no minimum holding.

The new poor group includes the original poor, all low-infrastructure wealthy households, and high-infrastructure wealthy households without sufficient gold capacity. The new wealthy group contains only high-infrastructure households with sufficient capacity. Non-hand-to-mouth households remain separate.

Matching joint shares and consumption estimates are not yet available in the results. The completed comparisons above retain their own definitions and samples.

How these estimates are constructed

BPP fits income and consumption second moments by minimum distance. The selected lagged-response version allows the consumption response to a temporary shock to extend into the next period. Commault uses future income growth; the choice of lead is displayed. Crawley–Kuchler compares consumption and income changes at the selected change lengths.

The sample consists of households with a 2019 classification and at least one member aged 25–64. Income and consumption must be positive. Total consumption excludes self-production. Income and consumption are CPI-deflated and divided by the household’s mean real monthly income, excluding 2020 from that mean. Observations outside the calendar-month 1st–99th percentiles of either ratio are removed. This is the Crawley–Kuchler trimming rule used across the three methods.

Annual observations require at least eight of twelve months. First stages are fitted separately within each sample window. The full sample retains the COVID period and includes COVID interactions. Estimation is unweighted; the source supplies household-clustered uncertainty. Counts therefore differ from survey-weighted classification populations.

The preferred combined housing-screen, infrastructure and gold-capacity partition has no matching estimate. The available low-infrastructure and gold-capacity groups are separate comparisons. Rupee MPCs are displayed only when available; no log coefficient is converted here.

The annual estimates describe income innovations inferred from the panel. Job loss offers a complementary view of a concrete household event.

Sample, timing and interpretation

Prior wage workers aged 25–62 are followed across interviews. The baseline retains zero consumption and zero wages. All eight financial months must be observed; the alternative sample requires positive consumption in every month. Total consumption excludes loan repayments and insurance premiums.

Interview windows sum the four months before the earlier interview and four months after the later interview, leaving out the intervening months. Calendar-wave comparisons instead use four-month means. Their timing and eligibility differ.

The unweighted two-stage least-squares model controls for age, age squared, sex, prior wages and state-by-endpoint-month effects (state-by-wave in the calendar-wave design). Uncertainty clusters by household. Groups use the fixed 2019 gold-illiquid classification, 75% pledgeable gold and no minimum holding. Group estimates require the later interview to be in January 2022 or after.

Pandemic windows from March 2020 through December 2021 are excluded. The sample retains repeated workers and concurrent household job losses; financial coverage ends in May 2025.

08The paper and its foundations

Follow the evidence further.

The research connects the composition of wealth to household financial constraints and consumption adjustment. Combining complementary surveys makes it possible to study those connections when no single survey observes every part of a household’s finances.

A glossary of the key ideasEight useful definitions
Liquid wealth
Resources that can be used readily to pay for current spending. Net liquid wealth subtracts the relevant debts.
Illiquid wealth
Assets such as housing and land that take a sale or borrowing arrangement to turn into spending money.
Hand-to-mouth (HtM)
The paper’s balance-sheet classification for households with limited liquidity relative to income. It is not a synonym for income poverty.
Gold collateral capacity
Whether the specified pledgeable value of gold can bridge a household’s measured liquid shortfall. This measures potential capacity, not observed borrowing.
Elasticity
The percentage change in consumption associated with a 1% income change in the specified estimation exercise.
Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)
The change in consumption, in rupees, associated with a one-rupee income change.
Transitory and permanent shocks
A transitory shock is a temporary income innovation. A permanent shock changes the continuing level of income in the estimated income process.
Standard error
A measure of the estimate’s statistical uncertainty. Exhibits retain the uncertainty supplied by their original results.
About these resultsHow to read the comparisons

This public release presents a fixed set of results. Each figure identifies its definition and sample.

The exhibits answer different questions about household finances. Household shares describe how common each group is, profiles describe the households within those groups, and consumption estimates describe responses to income changes.

Read each figure with its classification and sample. Shares use survey weights; consumption estimates use the sample and weighting stated in their section. A housing profile or an estimation sample can contain different households from the classification cross-section.

Elasticities and MPCs express responses in different units. Elasticities compare percentage changes; MPCs compare rupee changes. The charts label their uncertainty, and selections without a matching result show “Not yet available.”
